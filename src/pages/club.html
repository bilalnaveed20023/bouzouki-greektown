One room. Four corners.

Bouzouki is not a complex of floors and mezzanines. It is one dark room in the middle of Greektown, built around a stage, wrapped in a bar, and open six nights a week until two. Everything below is that room, corner by corner.

IThe Stage

Lit from three sides.

The stage sits centre-floor, not against a wall — which means there is no bad angle and no back of the room. Rail seating runs the full perimeter and it is first come, first served. On a Friday it is gone by ten.

Sets rotate continuously from doors to last call. There is no interval and no headline slot; the roster works through the night, so whenever you walk in, the room is already going.

Tipping the stage

Singles at the rail, and the house keeps a change machine and an ATM on site so you are never hunting for a cash back at the bar. Photography of any kind is not permitted on the floor — phones away, all night, no exceptions.

IIThe Bar

Ask for the pour,
not the menu.

Full liquor, cold domestic and import, and a bar crew that has worked this block longer than most of the restaurants on Monroe. Nothing on the back bar is decorative.

This is a bar first and a bar always: it is the reason the room still works at one in the morning when the rest of downtown has called it. Well pours, call brands, and a premium shelf for bottle service — and someone behind it who will tell you what to order.

IVThe Kitchen

Hot food at one in the morning.

Detroit closes early and eats late, which is an awkward combination everywhere except here. Our kitchen is on its feet until close: Greek plates from the room’s own history, wings, burgers, and platters built for a table of eight.

It is a genuine kitchen, not a fryer in a corner. The Greek side of the menu is the oldest thing in the building — a direct line back to when 432 E Lafayette was a restaurant with bouzouki players in it.

The name

Why “Bouzouki”?

A bouzouki is a long-necked Greek string instrument — the sound of a taverna at midnight. When this address opened as a Greek restaurant and lounge, that is exactly what it was: live strings, plates coming out of the kitchen, belly dancers working between courses, Greektown at full volume.

The neighbourhood turned over. The casino arrived. The stage changed shape and so did the entertainment. But nobody ever saw a reason to change the sign — and if you look at the club’s mark, the fluted column and the two dancers are still standing exactly where they have always stood.

Greektown’s oldest late room, under its original name.
